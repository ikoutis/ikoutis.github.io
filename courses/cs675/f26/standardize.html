Both walkers move at once, each with its own η: the same fraction of a different largest stable step. Past f = 1 a walker climbs instead of descending. “To the bottom” walks until the loss is within 1% of its minimum, or gives up after a million steps.

What to look at. Press to the bottom. The right map, standardized, is a circle: the downhill direction points straight at the bottom, and the walk is over in a few steps. The left map is a canyon. Its walls are so steep in a that the step size has to be tiny, and the same tiny step then crawls along the gently sloping floor in b for tens of thousands of steps.

Press centered. Subtracting the mean removes the tilt of the canyon: the contours stand upright, because the two parameters stop depending on each other. Press divided only: the canyon opens into an ellipse, but a tilted one. It takes both, c = mean and s = standard deviation, to make the circle. Drag the two sliders and watch the shape change continuously.

Move f past 1. Both walkers start climbing, each at its own scale: what makes a step too large is not its size in absolute terms but its size against the steepest wall of the bowl, 2/λmax. On a circle the walk at f = 0.5 lands on the bottom in exactly one step.

The loss of a line is L(a, b) = (1/n) Σ (a zi + b − lowi)², a quadratic in (a, b), so every contour is an ellipse whose axes come from the matrix of second derivatives, H = 2 [[mean z², mean z], [mean z, 1]]. Its eigenvalues λmax and λmin are the curvatures across and along the valley; their ratio κ is the condition number, and a contour's long axis is √κ times its short one. Gradient descent with step η converges when η < 2/λmax and then needs about κ steps to shrink its error by a fixed factor along the valley. With one standardized feature, mean z = 0 and mean z² = 1, so H = 2I: a circle, and η = 0.5 is Newton's step. The data: daily high and low at Newark Liberty airport, 2006–2024, from NOAA's GHCN-Daily record for station USW00014734, 6,940 days; here as 1,903 distinct pairs with their counts, which give the same loss. High: mean 65.0, standard deviation 18.5 °F. Notes: sections 3.3 (gradient descent), 3.5 (the update rules) and 3.7.2 (scaling features) of the Machine Learning notes.
