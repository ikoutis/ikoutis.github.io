A batch stands in for the data

Newark airport, 6,940 days from 2006 to 2024: the day's low against the day's high. The line that fits all of them is fixed. How close does a handful of days get to it?

What to look at. The black line is the least-squares line of all 6,940 days. Each red line is the least-squares line of one batch of days, the ones lit up. Draw a few batches with k = 4, then with k = 64, then k = 1024: the red lines crowd in on the black one. On the small map, every batch becomes a dot at its (a, b); the cloud of dots is how much a batch of that size can be wrong by.

A small batch already "knows" roughly where the line is. That is the whole reason gradient descent can afford to look at a few points per update.

What to look at. Each round uses only the next days (lit up) to compute the gradient, and moves the red line by one step. On the map, the red arrow is the step actually taken; the black arrow is the step that all 6,940 days would have taken from the same spot. With k = 1 the red arrow points anywhere; with k = 64 it nearly agrees with the black one, at a hundredth of the work.

One pass through all the batches is an epoch. Watch the loss curve: the small-batch path is noisy but gets many updates per epoch; the full-batch path is smooth but takes one.
